In smiths' usage, **Damascus** usually means **pattern-welded steel**. Several steels that etch differently are [[en/advanced/forge-welding|forge welded]] into a billet, then folded, twisted or stacked as a mosaic. Etching reveals the pattern.

> [!note] Terminology (current scholarship)
> Historical **"true" Damascus (wootz)** is a **crucible steel** from India / Central Asia. Its pattern comes from carbide bands *within a single* steel, not from welded layers. European **pattern welding** is a separate tradition (Migration-period blades, Viking-age swords). Both are loosely called "Damascus".

## Steel pairings (examples)
| Bright (nickel-bearing) | Dark | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 15N20 (≈ 75Ni8) | 1095 (≈ C100) | Classic, similar hardening temperatures |
| 15N20 | O2 (1.2842) | Very common in Central Europe |
| Pure iron / wrought iron | Carbon steel | Historical, "refined" steel |

## Basic sequence
1. Grind layers of equal size (remove scale), stack them, weld the ends (arc or forge) and add a handle bar.
2. **First weld** at welding heat with light, quick blows (hand, press, power hammer).
3. **Draw, cut, restack** (layer count multiplies: 7 → 14 → 28 → 56 …).
4. Patterning: **twist**, ladder (grooves), rain-drop/rose (drilled), mosaic (stacks of square bars).
5. Forge to shape, [[en/advanced/heat-treatment|heat treat]], grind, **etch** (dilute ferric chloride) and neutralize.

## Edge steel in axes (Nils Ögren)
Nils Ögren combines axe bodies of soft steel with pattern-welded or tool-steel edges. His **"hamburger technique"** is a variation for welding in edge steel; the name suggests a sandwich arrangement (edge steel between body material, comparable to *san mai*). The details are in the video. This description is inferred from the title and description, not from a transcript.
